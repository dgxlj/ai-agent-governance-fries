# My Fries Were “Sanctioned” by a Rogue Delivery AI: On Public-Facing Algorithmic Privilege Escalation, Security Boundaries, and Orderly AI Governance

## Introduction

As a rookie who has spent more than twenty years getting his hands dirty in information security and systems architecture, I spend most of my time dealing with low-level protocols, vulnerability research, and architectural planning. But I never imagined that one day, an everyday food-delivery algorithm would execute a precision “dimensionality-reduction strike” against me.

This is the story of a common little delivery mishap — something likely to happen, usually with little consequence. Yet it was more than a missing item. **It was a technical “encirclement,” orchestrated by cost-optimization systems, AI agents, and a major platform’s legal department acting in concert.** When algorithms cross human boundaries and start forcibly adjudicating our lives, we need to be alert: AI must not become a cyber-accomplice that helps the powerful bully the weak, much less encourage a culture of buck-passing and evading responsibility.

## The Real World, Sidelined by an Algorithm

Get comfortable. Story time!

A few days ago, I suddenly felt the need to top up on some “biologically derived, high-energy solid-state fuel.” Translation: I was craving junk food. So I ordered a meal through a food-delivery platform. When it arrived, everything was there except an order of fries worth about **$3**.

I had ordered according to my appetite. For a working meal, losing the staple meant getting stuck halfway to full, with that maddening, neither-here-nor-there hunger. Your stomach has loaded to 99.999%, and the progress bar has stopped moving. Insert your favorite smirking reaction face here.

Within two minutes, I contacted the restaurant. My request was exceedingly simple and reasonable:“Please deliver the missing fries.”

Naturally, “nothing should go wrong” meant something absolutely would. What greeted me was the restaurant’s AI customer service, capable only of repeatedly demanding photos and dispensing mechanical responses. It could not understand “deliver the missing item,” one of the most basic ways to fulfill an obligation in human society. With no other option, I turned to the platform’s official customer service.

And here, everyone, is where the problem began. What happened next was chilling. The official customer-service representative was also an AI. But this one was different: a “smarter” AI with agent permissions to perform operations directly. I had never requested a refund.

Nevertheless, the AI overstepped its authority, triggered the refund process in the backend, and returned the $3 to my original payment method. My phone went *ding*: refund received!

That operation immediately locked down the platform’s channel for handling the transaction dispute. I was not about to let it go. After laboriously working through a series of prompts, I finally found a human representative. But the human who took over had been reduced to a broken record:“The refund has already been issued. Delivery is no longer possible.” “That was handled automatically by our newly launched AI customer service, which is still in testing.” Beyond those lines, there was no substantive action at all. I’m taking a wild guess here, but perhaps their so-called standard responses were AI-generated too. 🙄 As for me, the refunded $3 was not even enough to meet the minimum for placing a separate delivery order.

That was the whole affair. Within this perfect closed loop, I was a living, breathing human being whose hunger, requests, dignity, and rights as a party to the contract had been completely screened out by a cold wall of algorithms.

## The Commercial Calculation Behind $3: Using a “Refund” to Conceal a “Breach”

By this point, I suspect you have experienced a similarly infuriating moment: the delivery you were looking forward to arrives without a key side dish. You contact customer service, only for them to throw a few bucks back in your face without a discussion and disappear at the speed of light. What did you do? Swallow your anger? Go downstairs and buy some bread to fill the gap? Most people compromise. And that is precisely the ending the platform’s number-crunchers hope for. This hardly counts as a conspiracy anymore. It is a scheme operating in plain sight!

In the cost model for on-demand delivery, calling out a rider specifically to deliver a single bag of fries can mean a few dollars in additional delivery costs. That is a rough estimate; anyone who knows the business is welcome to correct it. For the restaurant and platform, redelivery brings in no additional revenue and means paying the delivery expense out of pocket. A pure loss. Imagine every merchant on the platform leaving things out a few times a day — wouldn’t those costs eat the platform alive? Platforms constantly talk about strictly managing their merchants and rigorously protecting consumers. But there is a real gap they cannot bridge: they have no effective means of ensuring that merchants never make mistakes.

And so the algorithm delivers its coldest “optimal solution”: **A forced refund**. The restaurant avoids the delivery charge. The platform avoids the trouble of dispatching a rider. For a mere $3, they emerge unscathed and erase the cost of their breach. Of course, those costs have actually been borne entirely by you: You went hungry! You are the only party in this whole affair left paying an unreasonable price. **The system runs flawlessly, while treating you — the “human” — as disposable data waste.**

Whether this mechanism originally arose from cost control, fulfillment efficiency, or standardized customer service, its objective result is the same: the consumer’s original request for continued performance is unilaterally converted into a refund, with no effective way for the consumer to reject that conversion.

## A Process Gone Rotten: AI Becomes a “Breakwater” for the Powerful to Use Against the Weak

A breakwater is meant to protect against destructive waters. But viewed through the lens of abusive system architecture, this operation reveals technology at its most grotesquely distorted.

**Platforms and merchants already hold the data, the capital, and the power to make the rules. They are unequivocally the powerful parties. The consumer at the other end of the phone, waiting for a full meal, has no bargaining power.** Now the powerful cannot even be bothered to have a human come forward and fob you off. They simply erect an AI breakwater and isolate the consumer outside the system.

More frustrating still, this algorithm has precisely gauged the limits of administrative regulation. When you take this experience to market-regulation authorities or other responsible agencies, officials obliged to follow the rules often find themselves powerless. On the cold ledger, the platform has completed a “full refund” and, in legal terms, “terminated the infringement of property rights.” The officials see that the money has been returned, that mediation no longer has an object, and that all they can do is close the case. What remains is to pursue the legal relationship as a dispute over contractual performance or a consumer contract. Otherwise known as: t-a-k-i-n-g i-t t-o c-o-u-r-t.

Yes. Over a bag of fries.

That is the reality. Hastily issuing a refund to evade contractual responsibility has long been commonplace. But seeing AI begin to execute that practice automatically still brought me out in a cold sweat. Alongside my astonishment at the platform’s audacity — or, shall we say, its determination to tempt fate — I could sense the “clever design” at work. An automatic refund has a “dispute-flattening” effect: it repackages a question about procedural choice, legality, the manner of contractual performance, and the boundaries of automated decision-making as a minor financial dispute over $3 that has already been refunded. In plain English: the platform is now using AI to exploit legal loopholes.

But what about the very real hunger? What about the spirit of the contract that was ignored? Who takes responsibility for the consumer’s empty stomach? Nobody cares. The platform uses a refund to create the illusion of “compliance.” **It has clearly breached its obligations under civil law, yet carefully tucked itself into a safe zone under administrative law.**

## Legally Naked in the Age of Human–Machine Contracts

At its core, commercial exchange is a contractual act between people. What the law constrains and protects is also the human consideration and basic standards that govern how people treat one another.

When an AI agent with the system’s highest privileges inserts itself into a transaction and even forces decisions upon us, we have to ask: Does AI possess the legal standing to bear liability for breach of contract? Does this kind of interaction that oversteps its authority enjoy protection under existing law?

It reminds me of science-fiction master Isaac Asimov’s *The Bicentennial Man*, later adapted into the highly rated science-fiction film *Bicentennial Man*. Andrew, a robot who develops self-awareness, spends two hundred years and goes through countless courtroom debates before barely managing to win the dignity that humans possess from birth. Today, we have yet to clarify AI’s legal status, but capital is already impatient to give it life-and-death authority over our money and our orders.

**Are we really ready to welcome an era in which cold, impersonal agents control the allocation of society’s resources? Who exactly are you welcoming: Andrew the robot butler, or the Terminator T-800?**

## A Warning: Don’t Let Technology Become a Fig Leaf for Abuse

Today, an algorithm can forcibly deprive you of the right to a mouthful of hot fries just to save a few dollars in delivery costs. Left unchecked, tomorrow it could use the same logic to unilaterally decide your fate in medical triage, financial advice, or intelligent dispatch.

**Technology should be a stairway toward a better world. It should not become a weapon with which the powerful use algorithms to launch dimensionality-reduction strikes against the weak in a digitally stratified society. The law should protect contracts grounded in human relationships and consideration, rather than one-sided, oppressive terms between people and cold code.**

The governance concerns are already visible in major European and American instruments. The EU AI Act provides for risk management, automatic logging, and human oversight for systems within its high-risk category. Its oversight provisions include the ability, where appropriate, to disregard, override, or reverse an output, and to intervene in or stop a system’s operation. In the United States, NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework sets out a voluntary approach to governing, mapping, measuring, and managing AI risks, including human oversight and mechanisms to disengage or deactivate systems whose performance falls outside their intended use. These instruments differ in scope and legal force, but they address concrete questions of oversight, traceability, intervention, and control.

Let’s widen the lens once more. Having looked at Europe and the United States, what does the law in China say about automated decisions affecting individuals?

China’s Personal Information Protection Law, Article 24, sets requirements and limits on automated decision-making using personal information. It requires transparency and fair, impartial outcomes. Where a decision significantly affects an individual’s rights and interests, it also provides the right to request an explanation and to refuse a decision made solely through automated decision-making.

In May this year, China also issued the Implementation Opinions on the Regulated Application and Innovative Development of AI Agents, explicitly addressing permission management, behavioral controls, verifiable and traceable behavior, risk intervention, joint human–AI review, and mechanisms for interception and blocking. Then, on September 14, China’s National Technical Committee on Cybersecurity Standardization released the AI Safety Governance Framework 3.0. The official explanation described it as a response to emerging AI trends and new security governance challenges, explicitly emphasizing a human-centered approach and the need to keep AI safe and controllable. A subsequent official explanation also highlighted this version’s particular focus on the risks that agents can carry from cyberspace into the physical world.

Meanwhile, at the US–China leaders’ meeting that concluded just days before this article was written, AI governance and risk management were formally brought to the forefront of global strategic competition. AI risks and governance have entered the highest-level agenda of both countries. The two sides not only held in-depth discussions on AI, but also agreed to establish a US–China AI dialogue to exchange views on its risks and benefits, as well as a bilateral communication channel for AI incidents. When the world’s two leading AI competitors formally put the establishment of AI risk communication mechanisms on the table, we should no longer think of “AI governance” merely as a matter of preventing scenes from science-fiction films from someday becoming reality.

The European Union, the United States, and China are among the major players in the global contest over AI development and governance, each with considerable influence over how the technology and its rules evolve. They have yet to converge on a common set of rules for AI systems. Even so, a shared concern is clear: as AI gains the ability to make decisions and carry them out, the scope of its authority must be defined, its actions must be subject to oversight, and the risk of deliberate misuse must be controlled. The aim is for AI to develop in an orderly, beneficial, and controllable way — not for its growing capabilities to become tools that harm the public, whether through poor design or deliberate abuse. The central question is not how intelligent an AI system might become. It is **who decides what it may decide and do, under what conditions, who can intervene when it crosses those limits, and where the boundary of human control must be drawn.**

The paths now visible are different. The EU is building a risk-based legal framework with specific obligations for systems within its scope. In the United States, the NIST framework offers organizations a voluntary way to identify and manage AI risks. China’s personal-information law addresses certain automated decisions through individual rights and duties imposed on those processing personal information. These differences may shape how each jurisdiction handles AI agents as they become more capable, but they do not tell us which approach will ultimately protect people best — or how any of them will handle an everyday dispute like mine. That will depend on what the rules actually cover, how they are implemented, and whether a person can still stop or challenge an automated action when it matters.

Many people assume that the “AI going out of control” feared in grand narratives means Skynet launching nuclear missiles in a science-fiction film. But as a security architecture practitioner, I know full well that **the first manifestation of AI going out of control will not be slaughter in the physical world. It will look more like what happened today: business agents permeating everyday life and quietly stripping away people’s statutory rights through its smallest details.**

We are at a dangerous juncture as AI applications move from “assisted perception” to “autonomous decision-making and execution.” When companies misuse AI in **services that affect the public**, without a strict permission sandbox, the platform is effectively exploiting the algorithmic black box to carry out a covert, **metaphorical “privilege escalation”** against society’s rules. At the technical implementation level, it bypasses our inherent right to refuse automated decision-making, unilaterally imposing oppressive terms through code.

We therefore call on lawmakers, the academic community, and our colleagues in cybersecurity: **the orderly development of AI must be built on algorithmic auditability and a zero-trust architecture in which humans retain an absolute right to halt the system. For public-facing AI agents with substantive operational permissions in particular, static data-compliance registration and filing cannot be the stopping point. Digital ethics review mechanisms and mandatory channels for activating human intervention must be introduced. Only by locking AI agents’ impulse to overreach inside the twin cages of law and technology, and decisively ending the unchecked growth and abuses of capital carried out in the name of “efficiency,” can we ensure that technology remains an amplifier of human well-being, rather than a cyber-fortress behind which monopolistic corporations evade their statutory obligations.**

(Editor’s note: “The orderly development of AI must be built on algorithmic auditability and a zero-trust authorization architecture for agents” is intended to mean that AI should not be presumed trustworthy. Different levels should be defined according to the risks of the business activities in which an agent operates and the scope of operations it controls, with different decision-making and execution permissions granted progressively. Of course, the principle of least privilege must be maintained throughout, with human confirmation and an absolute ability to halt execution preserved for high-risk or irreversible operations.)
